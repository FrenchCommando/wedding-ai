# How Claude Saved My Marriage
*A keynote. Roughly six minutes. Delivered with total, unearned confidence.*

Stage directions in brackets. Slide cues in `[S#]`.

---

`[S1 — title, slow plasma]`

[Walk on. Stop. Look at the audience for one beat too long.]

Thank you. Thank you. Please. Sit. You're already sitting. Good. That's the first step.

My name is Marion, and eighteen months ago I was a woman with a fiancée, a Python interpreter, and a plan. The fiancée is called Léa. She's here. Table nine.

Only one of those three was on board with the plan.

`[S2 — "THE PLAN"]`

The plan was simple. We were getting married. Weddings have guests, and guests have dietary requirements, and dietary requirements have opinions, and opinions have plus-ones. This is a graph problem. I said that out loud. At dinner.

Léa said: "It's a spreadsheet."

And I said the sentence that every relationship counsellor tells you not to say. I said:

"You know, if you learned to code, you'd see it."

[Pause.]

I have said that sentence... a lot. I have said it about the thermostat. I have said it about a lasagna. I once said it about a *dream she had*.

`[S3 — "she is reluctant" in tiny type, enormous negative space]`

She is, and I quote the wedding vows we have not yet exchanged, "reluctant."

She doesn't want to learn to code. She wants the thing to work. She wants to type a name and have the name be in the place where names go. She does not want to know about the place. She does not want to *name* the place.

And I love her. And I could not stop.

`[S4 — "THE INCIDENT"]`

So one night in February, she's asleep, I'm awake, and I have a beautiful thought. The thought is: what if I just... build it. The whole thing. Guest list, RSVP, seating chart, budget, vendor tracker. And what if I don't write it. What if I *describe* it.

[Lean in.]

I opened a terminal. I typed: "Build me a website to manage my wedding." And I hit enter.

And at 2:47 a.m. I had a website.

At 3:10 a.m. it had a seating chart with drag-and-drop.

At 3:40 a.m. it knew that her uncle Gary cannot sit near her uncle Randy, because I typed "Gary cannot sit near Randy," and it just... did the thing. No `for` loop. No graph. No sentence.

`[S5 — the psychedelic bit: "IT JUST DID THE THING", background goes full kaleidoscope]`

[This is the Reggie Watts moment. Voice drops. Loop pedal energy, but with words.]

Now. I want to talk about what happened in my body.

Because for fifteen years, "learn to code" was a door. And I was on one side, and she was on the other side, and I kept saying: *come through the door, it's nice in here, we have dictionaries.*

And at 3:40 in the morning I realised.

There's no door.

There was never a door. There's a *curtain.* And you can just... talk through the curtain. You say "Gary, Randy, keep them apart," and the curtain says "done," and it's done.

I had spent our entire relationship guarding a door that was a curtain.

[Let that sit. Sip water you don't need.]

`[S6 — "THE MORNING AFTER"]`

Léa comes down at 7. I'm still there. I have the face. You know the face. The face of a woman who has seen the curtain.

I turn the laptop around.

She types her cousin's name. The cousin goes in the place where cousins go. She types "vegan, but not the annoying kind." It goes in the place where that goes. She drags her mother to table one and her mother's boyfriend to table nine.

And she looks at me, and she says — and I will remember this sentence for the rest of my life — she says:

"Okay. This is actually fine."

[Beat.]

"This is actually fine" is the highest rating my work has ever received. From anyone. Including code review.

`[S7 — "WHAT I LEARNED"]`

So. What did I learn. Because it's a keynote, and the contract says I have to learn something.

One. She was right. It *was* a spreadsheet. It was a spreadsheet with a good UI. She was describing the product and I was describing the implementation, and we were both correct, and only one of us was annoying.

Two. "Learn to code" was never about code. It was about wanting her to be in the room where I am. And it turns out the room has a second entrance. It's a text box.

Three. The thing I built at 3 a.m. — she uses it every day. She's added three features. She doesn't know they're features. She thinks she's "asking it to do stuff." And I have made a decision, as a wife-to-be, not to correct her.

`[S8 — "SHE STILL WON'T LEARN TO CODE"]`

She still won't learn to code.

I've made peace with it. Mostly. I'm ninety percent at peace. The other ten percent is currently building her a Flutter app she didn't ask for.

`[S9 — the close: "Thank you. Table nine." with slow fade]`

The wedding is in October. Two hundred and twelve guests. Gary and Randy are on opposite sides of the room. Nobody wrote a graph.

Thank you. I'm at table nine.

[Walk off before the applause peaks. That's the trick.]
